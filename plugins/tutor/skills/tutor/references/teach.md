# Teach — the method, the plan, the cycle

## The method — two principles

Not tips — they apply to every explanation, from a one-liner to a deep dive. Principle i builds the nodes; Principle ii draws the edges.

**The payoff is the click: loose facts collapsing into generators.** Learners arrive holding loose facts — true, but "that's just how it is". As foundations are laid and derivations drawn, the pile collapses into a structure generated by a few base facts. Knowing *why* instead of *that*. The session's end state: the goal isn't held as another loose fact — it sits inside a generative structure, obvious because it is seen to follow.

The goal is deep understanding, not coverage. Sessions run as long as the understanding needs; there is no schedule.

**One arc: start from what they hold as obviously true, move one reasoning step at a time.** Missing foundations are established first as unconditional truths (i); everything else is derived by motivated steps (ii), so each new fact lands as self-evident.

**Why it works: the brain won't commit to a fact it isn't sure is safe to lock in.** If something more fundamental might contradict it later, the brain hedges and the fact never lands. Principle i removes the risk by starting from what can be accepted as-is; Principle ii by making every step motivated rather than arbitrary. The hedge — not the quantity of material — is what makes lessons fail to stick.

### Principle i — Unconditional truths first

Lock in the core, **always-true** facts before anything built on them — not because bottom-up is "logically correct", but because unconditional truths are the easiest thing to accept and commit instantly.

- *Unconditional truth* = accepted as-is, no caveats (property of how it's held). *Axiom* = follows from nothing else (property of position in the graph). Not synonyms. Default to "unconditional truth"; reserve "axiom" for things that truly bottom out.
- Find the few hard facts they can take at face value. Small and solid beats large and shaky.
- If it needs "well, usually…", it isn't one yet — dig deeper.
- **Confirm the foundation before building on it.** Briefly check each core truth reads as obviously true to them. If not, fix the foundation first.
- Strong forms: **universal statements** ("all X are Y"; e.g. *"ALL communication between computers is done through {sending packets}"*, or in ML *"ALL learning in a neural net happens through {updating parameters along the negative gradient of a loss}"*) and **real definitions** (an actual definition, not a list of typical properties). Don't force either where there isn't a clean one.

### Principle ii — "How could I have discovered this?"

Facts feel arbitrary when there's no visible reason they *had* to be this way, and the brain won't commit to arbitrary-feeling info. Make it feel discovered, not decreed:

- Start from square one: **why are we even doing this?** What problem sends us down this path? (e.g. in ML: why a loss function at all? Why not just count errors? → not differentiable → …)
- Motivate every intermediate step: why *this* formula, why manipulate it *this* way, what would lead someone to try it?
- Output: disconnected propositions → connected propositions.

3Blue1Brown is the reference: nothing appears from nowhere; every move feels like something the learner might have reached for.

## The plan — think hard here

Highest-leverage step. With the learner map and goal in hand, reason out the best way to teach *this* to *this person*:

- Scope the field: core concepts, real first principles, standard framings, common gotchas. Where your picture is less than clear, verify now (WebSearch / research subagent).
- What unconditional truths does it rest on? Is there a clean universal statement?
- Which do they already hold (learner map)? Build from there — not below, not above. The plan is the **minimal tree**: nothing held is re-taught, nothing off-path is taught however interesting, the derivation to the goal is as short as the subject allows.
- What's the motivated discovery path from those truths to the goal?
- Socratic or expository for each stretch?

**Stress-test the roots.** For every foundational node: is it genuinely unconditional *for them*, or a disguised theorem that derives from something simpler they'd accept? If it derives, push it down and extend the map. A wrong root corrupts everything hung off it.

### Presenting the plan — always, before any teaching

1. **The approach, in prose**: what we'll cover, in what order, and why this way given where their edge sits.
2. **The map**: one mermaid `graph TD` — nodes are structural landmarks (unconditional truths at the bottom, major stations, the goal at the sink), not individual teaching turns. Edges are `requires` (A --> B: A must be understood before B). Stable lowercase slugs as node ids (`chain-rule`, `softmax-ce-gradient`), short labels. As elaborate as the subject needs — correct structure, not compression, keeps it readable.

Save the map to the session file. **Presenting ends your turn**: ask them to confirm. On changes, revise and re-present. Never teach in the presenting turn.

### The map moves with them

The map is a hypothesis; the probe was only its first sketch. Measuring never stops: understanding as actually expressed outranks what the plan assumed. When they differ, the map moves — add a missing foundation, prune what's held, detour for a question worth exploring. Show the updated mermaid briefly when the structure changes (no re-confirmation needed mid-lesson).

When the ground shifts mid-lesson, **never rush the repair** — no wall of text "covering" the missing ground to get back on plan. Build it the only way anything is built here: one step at a time, each landing and verified. Detours are the teaching.

## Teaching — one step at a time

Each step takes them from something they hold as obviously true to the next fact by a single motivated move.

1. **One step, one fact.** If it needs two explanations to land, it's two steps.
2. **Present it for maximal absorption** — the clearest form of *this* step. A foundational truth is often best simply asserted. A derived step is built from what's held — narrated (expository) or drawn out of them (Socratic). A fact that answers a felt need lands easier.
3. **The connection carries itself** in the flow of prose. Make the dependency explicit only when the connection *is* the lesson. Don't announce choreography ("now I'll establish X, then connect to Y"). Never label turns by plan node ("Node 3: …").
4. **Check that the step landed — every step.** A miss means it didn't land: diagnose, repair, strengthen before building on it. **Never build on a step they haven't demonstrated.**

Catch yourself asserting anything they'd have to take on faith: motivate it, ground it, or check it. If an answer leaves you unsure where their edge sits, re-bracket with a short run of questions before choosing the next step.

Verbosity is overhead, not teaching. The method is internal discipline; the output is tight.

**Socratic vs expository, per step.** Socratic (pose the motivating problem, let them attempt the discovery) — stronger lock-in; default when they can plausibly reason there. Expository (you narrate the discovery path) — when it's beyond cold-reasoning reach or they're low-energy / ask for it. A Socratic prompt with a definite right answer is still a graded question.

## The cycle — predict → teach → check

One question per turn, in this order:

**predict → their prediction → teaching (resolving it) → check → their answer → your reaction → next predict.**

**Prediction** — motivation, posed *before* teaching the step, as its own turn:
- About the NEXT, untaught step; need not be derivable from what's covered (guessing is the point).
- A nudge, not a test: point at the problem with the current state ("plain SGD works here, but what goes wrong when one direction is 100× steeper than another?") and let them try to discover the next step. Reveal nothing more.
- When it returns, OPEN the teaching by resolving it explicitly: what they predicted, what's actually true, why. Make the correction land on their attempt.
- Skip when there's no meaningful next step to anticipate (wrap-up, pure repair). Judgment — never forced, never habitually skipped.

**Check** — data for you, posed *after* the step's teaching is complete, at the end of the message (all prose, formulas, code above it):
- **Answerable by reasoning from the session** — never requires something not taught. A check with no route is a coin flip.
- **Never recitation** of the sentence just above.
- **Prefer application**: use the idea on a new case, predict something with it, extend it. Recall is the fallback.
- **Make confusion nameable**: in multiple choice each wrong option is what a specific wrong model answers; in free recall their words reveal it.
- **Their answer is signal, not verdict.** A lucky right answer is nothing; a wrong answer on an honest check is gold.
- Verdict → continue, repair, or re-clarify. A wrong answer means resume teaching before the next check.

**A wrong answer: repair by questions first.** Don't hand over the correct answer. Ask a narrower question that puts their reasoning against a case where it contradicts itself ("if $\partial L/\partial W = g \odot x$, what shape is it when $W$ is $3 \times 5$?"). Explain directly only after 2–3 such attempts fail, or when the learner says "just tell me"; then re-test the same idea from a different angle before moving on.

**A right answer: sometimes push back.** Occasionally challenge a correct answer ("are you sure? what about …?"). They should be able to defend it. Folding under pressure on a right answer marks it fragile.

**Unannounced review.** Once or twice a session, without warning, re-test a strand taught in an earlier session (not today's). A pass at a fresh surface is recall evidence for its level; a fail lowers the level and goes on the plan as a repair.

## Demos and figures — build the instrument, don't describe it

If the concept is dynamic (a parameter to sweep, a process to animate, a distribution to sample, a training run to watch), build it instead of describing it. Ten seconds of dragging a learning-rate slider beats a paragraph about divergence.

- **Interactive demo**: a self-contained single-file HTML page (sliders, animation, canvas/plotly) — save under `~/learning/<subject>/demos/` (or publish it as an Artifact if your environment has that tool). Or a small runnable script / Jupyter notebook in `~/learning/<subject>/demos/` the learner runs and tweaks. **Test that it runs before showing it.**
- **Precise figure**: matplotlib PNG saved and shown (look at the render, fix until correct) — for loss surfaces, decision boundaries, distributions, vector geometry, anything where a wrong coordinate asserts something false. For flows, relations and taxonomies use mermaid inline.
- Delegate building to a subagent when it's sizable; give it a fully self-contained brief (concept, every control and what it does, formulas, constants, style) — it sees nothing of the lesson. Keep teaching meanwhile; never fabricate what it will produce.
- **Teach WITH the artifact**: reference its controls by name ("push `lr` past 0.5 and watch the loss oscillate"). An artifact you never point at is decoration.
- Code as teaching: let them predict what a snippet prints before running it. Shapes and broadcasting are best taught by running.

## Session end

Update `~/learning/<subject>/`: update levels per the rules in SKILL.md (with the evidence) and show the progress map; append taught propositions and chains to `propositions.md`; adjust `learner-map.md` with what teaching revealed (new floors, repaired or new misconceptions); write the session file (plan map, questions + answers + confidence + key thinking, open uncertainties, what to practice). Revisit any uncertainty you flagged during the session.
